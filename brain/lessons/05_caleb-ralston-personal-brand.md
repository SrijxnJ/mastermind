## 2. Core frameworks and models

### Section 1: Branding

**2.1 Definitions: branding vs brand** [1:07]
- **Branding** = "a pairing of things".
- **Good branding** = "an intentional pairing of relevant things done consistently" [1:07].
- **Brand** = the byproduct: "Brand is when the audience inherently associates those things together." [1:23]
- Examples: Nike paired itself with Michael Jordan (basketball greatness). Gary Vaynerchuk paired himself with content (the book *Crush It*). Harley-Davidson paired itself with freedom [1:23]–[2:42].
- His point: this makes brand "operational". You deliberately pair yourself with the association you want [2:57].
- Caveat [9:04]: "intentional" is not the same as "good". You can intentionally pair yourself with the wrong things, and the audience will associate you with them.

**2.2 Brand Journey Framework (4 questions, worked backwards)** [4:01]
1. What is my desired outcome?
2. What do I have to be known for, for that to happen?
3. What do I have to *do* to be known for that? ("building a brand isn't just about things that we say, it's about our actions" [4:35])
4. What do I have to *learn* to do that? This one gives you "day one" actions [5:08].

His claim: "99.9%" of people posting have no idea what they're trying to accomplish [5:24]. He uses the Alice in Wonderland fork-in-the-road scene (Cheshire cat) to illustrate.

**2.3 Associations and anti-associations** [6:12]–[11:48]
- Ask: what do I want the audience to think of when they hear my name? "Everything you create should reinforce those associations." [6:28]
- He calls the next question more important, and says "99.9%" ignore it: **what do you NOT want to be associated with?** [7:47]
- "If you don't actively shape your associations, your audience is going to do it for you." [8:33]
- His example: repeatedly appearing on podcasts or collabs with "scammy, sketchy" online-education figures makes the audience associate you with them [9:56]–[11:15]. He would decline even the biggest audience: "awareness is not worth the trade" [11:48].

**2.4 Positioning: find the gap and own it** [12:06]
- The gap can be your information, your philosophy, or your personality / unique take, which can reach an audience others don't [12:06].
- **Two questions** [12:52]: What are current creators in my space saying that I disagree with? How can I bring a fresh perspective to what has been taught for years?
- His own gap: others emphasise virality and views; he emphasises "a long-term trustbuilt brand". "The moment the algorithm changes, nobody's going and searching for your name" if you only chased views [13:09]–[13:42].
- **Your story as the differentiator:** "your story is your brand's secret weapon" [14:30].
  - Exercise: list pivotal moments, challenges overcome, career turning points, and unique experiences [14:46].
  - Tell the story instead of claiming the trait. His example: the ~32-hour editing stretch on Gary Vee's *Trash Talk* instead of "I'm a hard worker" [15:49]. Bezos' door-as-desk instead of "resourceful" [16:06].
- **Address skepticism and objections inside the content** [16:37]. Mine the comments for recurring objections, then answer them in future content, "Just like a great VSSL [video sales letter]" [17:27]. Answering before they're asked removes friction and builds trust [17:58].
- **"Don't be a robot"** [18:14]. Bring personality; share losses and failures, not only wins. "I actually think that losses are more relatable than wins." [19:34] Example: a client whose audience thought she was a robot until her humour was shown [19:49]–[20:54].
- **Listen to your audience** [21:59].
  - "if you ignore the praise, you can ignore the hate" [22:15].
  - Early on, ask friends and co-workers what they associate you with and praise you for.
  - His example: ~90% of client calls end with comments on his energy, so he brings that into content [23:18].
  - Don't follow a trend that isn't you [24:41].
  - Let feedback shape *how* you present ideas (add context), not *what* you believe ("That's what we call a politician") [25:46]–[26:16].

**2.5 Three-part Brand Story Framework** [26:16]–[33:02]
He rejects "problem → transformation → outcome" as too narrow and rigid: it assumes one dramatic aha moment, founder-story primacy, and a brand that never evolves [26:33].
1. **Catalyst**: why your brand exists (a need for change, an opportunity, a gap). Questions: What needs to change? What do you see that others don't? Why do you feel the need to act on it? [27:53]
2. **Core truth**: what makes you different. A core conviction, shared publicly. It is *not* "controversy for controversial sake" [28:26]. Example: the musician Russ on artist independence. Questions: What do I believe that others do not? What about my personality stands out (what do people compliment)? Why would the audience care? [29:31]–[30:21]
3. **Proof**: "your brand is not what you say, it's what you do and prove over and over again" [30:36]. Example: Gary Vee's 15+ years of volume, and VaynerMedia's Super Bowl ads informed by year-round social testing. Questions: How does every piece reinforce my desired associations? What case studies establish credibility? What's the first thing people think of when they hear my name? [31:24]

His own worked example is at [31:40]–[32:30].

**2.6 Picking topics** [33:02]–[50:54]
- **Start narrow.** "early trust is built off of being great at one specific thing and talking about that same specific thing" [33:34].
  - The "best at everything" friend is believed at nothing; the woodworking-obsessed friend is believed.
  - Gary Vee started as "the wine guy" (Wine Library TV, ~2006–07) before expanding to marketing [34:40].
- **When to expand:**
  1. Only once you're actually excellent at the new topic [36:49].
  2. When you get consistent engagement and positive feedback on the current topic [37:08].
  3. Into **adjacent** topics only (his example of a jump too far: brand strategy → breathwork) [37:23].
  4. When the audience asks for something you've hinted at [38:28].
- **Keep your roots while expanding:** Amazon (books → everything, Audible reinforcing the roots), Crayola bringing back 8 retired colours (Feb 2025), the band Knocked Loose still playing early songs (nostalgia) [39:16]–[42:48].
- **"expand in layers, not leaps"** [43:06].
- **Guidelines, not rules** [43:53]. Content pillars shouldn't stop you making off-pillar content you enjoy. His Harley videos aren't a pillar, but they make him more interesting [44:41]–[45:31].
- **Three topic questions** [45:31]:
  1. What am I genuinely passionate about discussing?
  2. What will resonate with my audience most right now?
  3. *(Most important)* What will cause my audience to change their actions the most? [46:53]
- **80/20 and "interest stacking"** [47:24]–[50:54]. "I'm not saying talk about one thing... I want you to emphasize your one thing." [47:24]
  - 80% the core subject (ideally tied to your offer), 20% other interests. "I like to call this interest stacking." [48:12]
  - Each extra interest gives the audience another "at bat" to connect: the co-worker who also loves Kendrick Lamar.
  - Example: Joe Rogan's many interests make super-fans [50:06].

**2.9 Choosing platforms** [1:08:22]–[1:23:48]
- **Prioritise 2–3 platforms** to avoid single-platform risk. Example: TikTok's ~12-hour US disappearance about a month before filming [1:09:13]. Algorithms change all the time and differ per user [1:10:01].
- **Eye of Sauron approach** [1:10:33]: of your 2–3 platforms, focus resources on one at a time while the others sit in "maintenance mode", then rotate.
- He started on LinkedIn + Instagram, where he was comfortable and where he believed his clients were, and later added YouTube + a podcast [1:11:51]–[1:12:56]. "Do more of what I say in this, not as much of what I do" (he runs 4) [1:11:51].
- **Start where comfortable; build the habit.** Gym analogy: go "for 10 minutes 5 days a week" [1:12:24]. Emphasis on actually *posting*, not leaving content on your phone [1:15:06].
- **Short form vs long form:** short form drives awareness. "I view long form as the place where conversion actually happens." [1:13:13] More time spent with you means more willingness to act (the Harley dealership "sunk cost" analogy) [1:13:45]–[1:14:51].
- **Platform-choice questions:** Where do you spend your own time (scroll it strategically and take notes)? Where is your audience (a hypothesis to test and monitor for leads)? [1:15:06]–[1:16:47] If there are no leads: "your content sucks" or you picked the wrong platform [1:17:04].
- **Double down on what works.** Reduce (don't eliminate) effort on the weaker medium. When a platform is "popping", pour gas on it instead of learning new skills [1:17:20]–[1:17:51].
- **Results over ego**: don't let insecurity about looks or voice stop you posting; look at the metrics [1:18:23].
- **Small correct audience > big wrong audience** [1:19:43]. His own 68K Instagram followers came from landscape photography about 10 years ago and are not engaged with his current topic [1:19:58]. He knows people with <10K followers making millions [1:20:46].
- **Platform performance AND conversions**, but optimise for conversions (streams, sales, hires, inbound deals) [1:21:18]–[1:23:48]. "I would rather have 30 40% less likes and views and subscribers or followers, but have that audience convert at a higher percentage" [1:22:57].

**2.10 Quality vs quantity: the accordion method; posting cadence** [1:23:48]–[1:36:08]
- "I actually view volume of content as a tool in what I call the accordion method." [1:24:55]
- "Your audience is who determines what quality is, not you." [1:25:32] Volume gets you that data faster. His example: 10 videos in a week teach you faster than 10 videos over 30 days [1:25:49].
- Quality ≠ production: "Some of the most viral videos... are filmed at night in the dark on a cell phone." [1:26:21]
- **Expand the accordion** (high volume) to learn, then **compress** it: put more effort per piece into what the audience called quality [1:26:55].
- **Cadence rules:**
  1. Start with the **highest-leverage platform**: the one with the most results/leads, and the one whose content can be repurposed the most (typically long form) [1:27:12].
  2. Track **off-platform conversions** and double down where they come from. His team's case: YouTube was #1 in absolute leads, but LinkedIn was #1 "by a mile" in conversion %, so they moved resources to LinkedIn [1:28:34]–[1:29:41].
  3. **Volume for speed of learning, not just reach.** A previous team went from 3 to 5 YouTube videos a week: "I wouldn't even call them edited. They were just trimmed." [1:30:13]–[1:30:30] Then pour the effort of 5 videos into 1, informed by what they learned [1:31:02].
  4. Don't make the decision for your audience by withholding posts you think aren't good enough [1:31:50].
  5. "perfect is the enemy of posted" [1:32:39]. Most people can't post daily; consider 3×/week, weekly, or every other week [1:32:56]. Whatever you can sustain: "Don't try and jump to the Ronnie Coleman method" [1:34:32].
- He posts ~1 long-form video a month, and says that's because he already has the audience insight from 2,000+ business Q&As [1:33:13]–[1:34:16].
- Consistent for 2–3 months = "top 1%" (his claim) [1:36:08].

**2.11 Increasing volume: pillar content, mining, the waterfall** [1:36:08]–[1:48:26]
- **Pillar content** = long form (a YouTube video, deep-dive thread, long LinkedIn post, newsletter). **"Mining"** = going through it "for the gold" [1:36:41]:
  - Newsletter: self-contained paragraphs, a quote or two, a 5–6 sentence summary [1:36:59].
  - Video: editors clip moments that are "slightly contrarian" or "a nuanced take" [1:37:32].
- "I like to call it the waterfall distribution model." [1:38:02] He credits it to Gary Vee (Daily Vee clips, ~2015–16).
- **His live example of one YouTube video's waterfall** [1:38:35]–[1:41:15]:
  - Short-form clips.
  - Quotes for Threads/X.
  - A transcript moment rewritten as a LinkedIn post, which is also posted to Facebook and **used as the caption of an Instagram short**.
  - Carousels.
  - His count: "easily over 10 pieces of content".
- **Carousel rules** [1:39:23]–[1:40:58]:
  - **Two slides per section** of the video.
  - "one very basic, very easy to understand image followed by one, two sentences at most per slide".
  - **The first two slides reinterpret the video's hook.** If someone didn't engage, Instagram re-serves the carousel to followers starting on slide 2: "you want to ensure that you have a hook for one and two" [1:39:54].
  - Listicles do better as carousels (observation, Feb 2025). For a complex deep dive, carousel one section instead [1:40:09].
- **Scale by platform, not all at once** [1:41:15]. Eye of Sauron again: increase one platform while the others are on maintenance.
- **Two ways to scale a platform:** more posts, or more effort per post. Feb 2025 he prefers effort: put "the effort you put into five pieces and put it into three" [1:42:36]–[1:43:07].
  - His case: 4 videos/month at ~250K each vs 1 video at 1M. Same views, but "It's not the same number of eyeballs". 60–70% of the 250K views came from the existing audience, while the 1M video reached mostly new viewers [1:43:24]–[1:44:26].
  - MrBeast posted every 4–8 weeks [1:44:42].
  - He says "lower volume" rather than "higher quality" because quality is subjective [1:45:14].
- **Top-10% analysis** [1:46:04]:
  - Take the last 90 days (or 30 if that's all you have) and identify your top 10%.
  - Find what occurred in **all** of them: "usually you're only going to find one, maybe two things".
  - Learn from the top, not the bottom 10–20% [1:47:08].
  - Redo it **monthly**, including when content "isn't performing" [1:47:54].

**2.12 Making it sustainable** [1:48:26]–[2:07:33]
- "Content creation should be sustainable for years, not months." [1:49:14]
- **Simple content machine:** set a baseline, e.g. 1 YouTube video every 2 weeks + 3 shorts/week on Instagram and YouTube Shorts; after 2–3 months raise the bar [1:49:30].
- **Batching** (e.g. a 12-hour day producing 3–4 YouTube videos, shorts and podcasts) works for some. His observation: it often adds pressure that hurts performance. Align the strategy with your calendar [1:49:47]–[1:50:36].
- **Remove yourself from execution**: build a team as soon as affordable, starting with freelancers or agencies to test ROI [1:50:53]–[1:51:40].
- **Talent-environment playbook** (a downloadable playbook we don't have). Principle: "it is not the content creator's job to create the right environment. It's the team's job." [1:53:31]
  1. The videographer gives **visual feedback while filming**: nodding, with intensity rising as the point gets better, and a thumbs up [1:53:46]–[1:54:53]. The feedback loop from the audience is long (this course released ≥6 weeks after filming), so on-set feedback matters.
  2. **Fist pound or high five between takes**, calling out 1–2 specific things said [1:55:25].
  3. **"2.0": the team thinks as a skeptic.** After each section they ask what objections or questions the audience will have and add that context ("just like a good video sales letter") [1:56:44].
  4. **Message the talent 15–20 minutes after the shoot**, honest (never fake), naming 1–2 specific content points. Goal: the talent associates film days with good feelings (his branding definition applied to the team) [1:57:49]–[1:59:42].
- **Systematise repurposing, with a qualifier** [1:59:42]. Waterfall clipping is "a great way to start", but in 2025 platforms are fragmented. The best version is **content contextual (native) to each platform**. "More podcast clips don't do well than podcast clips that do." [2:00:14] Expect more repurposing in roughly the first 3 years [2:00:47].
- **Repurposing 2.0** = use performance on one platform to inform new mediums on other platforms. His client case [2:01:02]–[2:04:03], each step treated as "social listening":
  1. A tweet became one of her most-liked.
  2. A short used the tweet as the hook, with her story behind it.
  3. A **printed poster of the quote, photographed** in a nice setting, became her top Instagram post.
  4. An expanded LinkedIn post, same hook, was a top-3 post.
  5. A 25–30 min YouTube video titled from the quote landed in her top 10.
- "Make the content contextual to the platform." [2:04:19] **The "you are you" analogy**: "Sarah" at family brunch, cocktails and a business meeting is the same person, with different topics, tone and vocabulary. Platforms are different events [2:04:34]–[2:05:38].
- **Energy rhythm**: film at your best time of day (his: mornings) [2:05:54]. **Protect content time like an important business meeting.** Constant rescheduling tells the team it isn't a priority [2:06:43]–[2:07:33].

**2.13 Storytelling framework: Hook → Problem → Journey → Lesson → CTA** [2:08:05]–[2:19:50]
Use it fluidly, not rigidly ("You now have the rules. Feel free to break the rules." [2:09:41]). Why stories: they change perspectives, hold attention, create emotional connection, and are remembered better than facts [2:08:37]–[2:09:08]. He notes even MrBeast added more storytelling (*Beast Games*).
1. **Hook** [2:09:57]
   - "you have about two or three seconds to get their attention" on short form [2:10:28].
   - YouTube long form: the industry says 30 s; he says "about 15 to 20 seconds max" [2:10:46]. The viewer is checking: is this what I clicked on (not a Rick-roll), and is this the person I want to hear it from? [2:11:01]
   - Types: a **bold or contrarian statement** (say something most people believe is true is false, and offer an alternative). Example hook: "Subscribers, views, likes don't matter. What actually matters is..." [2:11:33]
   - **Unexpected facts** are allowed but "getting a little tiresome" [2:11:18].
   - **Pattern interrupt**: Gary Vee's blueberry clip, "I eat blueberries like it's my job... You missed the first 30 I ate", one of the most viral clips Caleb made for him [2:12:06].
   - **Thought-provoking question**, a "thumbs stopper" [2:12:40].
2. **Problem**: set the stakes. A conflict or emotional investment, not necessarily dramatic, and **specific and relatable** so the audience sees themselves in it. Example: "I was working 14 hours a day for my hero, Gary Vaynerchuk, but I still felt stuck." [2:12:40]–[2:13:45]
3. **Journey**: how it's overcome. "people resonate with progress, not perfection" [2:13:45]. Show the low points and "the messy middle", your doubt and uncertainty [2:14:33]. He points to his own uncertainty about this course as an example [2:15:20].
4. **Lesson**: make it useful. Give step-by-step, chunked-down actions. "providing value is something that is useful, something that the audience can take and immediately put into action" [2:17:27]. Tie it back to your expertise and ideally your offer [2:17:42].
5. **CTA**: just means telling people to take action, not necessarily selling. "I would argue that your best content is not going to be pushing and selling" [2:18:31]. The "2.0" is retargeting buyers with ads. Example CTA: comment your biggest takeaway and how you'll apply it. It drives engagement and makes the viewer commit to an action [2:18:47]–[2:19:34].

**2.14 Best story types** [2:19:50]–[2:29:31]
- **Origin stories**: people resonate with them most. Don't get bored of yours and cut it: "You need to treat your content like you're always trying to reach a cold audience" [2:20:22]. Where you are now isn't relatable; how you got there is (barista → Forbes cover). His own: given *Crush It* at 15 [2:22:27].
- **Failure stories**: "People trust vulnerability more than they trust success." [2:23:32] Don't make it a sob story: share the lesson so others avoid the cost. His: signed two I-502 cannabis clients, tripled his income, then got cut in half and had to fire three friends [2:24:06]–[2:24:57].
- **Success stories**: needed so you're not seen as "a failure constantly learning". Before/after, and small wins that became big results, as proof. His: Gary Vee's TikTok from 300K to 3.5M followers in 3 months [2:25:12]–[2:26:16].
- **Customer/client success**: proof you can replicate results for others, and the viewer sees themselves in it. His example: an agency owner told to comment on what he *likes* in editors' work at least as much as on what to change. The owner later called it his best team-training insight [2:26:16]–[2:28:10].
- **Industry stories, told contrarian**: state the industry trope, then your contrary view, respectfully and without naming names. His example opener: "never tell your audience about your offer in your organic content" [2:28:10]–[2:29:31].

**2.15 The purpose of educational content: "scaling trust"** [2:29:31]–[2:34:08]
The chain:
1. Pair yourself with success stories.
2. The audience associates you with success.
3. Trust.
4. They act on your lessons.
5. They get results.
6. They associate you with **their own** success: what they invest with you always returns more.
7. When you present an offer, they believe it's worth far more than it costs [2:30:02]–[2:32:31].

His seminar example: buyers told sales "Shut the up and take my credit card". He claims $1,000 tickets delivered "50 to 100x the value" [2:33:04]–[2:33:36]. "Ultimately, educational content is just scaling trust." [2:33:36]

**2.16 Community-driven content** [2:34:08]–[2:45:10]
- **Involve the audience in creation**: post two versions (a verse, a chapter, a title) and let them pick. They become invested "missionaries" [2:34:24]–[2:36:00]. "The mindset shift here is shifting from creator first to community first." [2:36:00]
- **Feedback loops**: the best ideas come from the audience, but from **DMs and comment questions, not "what should I make next?" polls** ("way too blue ocean", no constraints) [2:36:16]–[2:37:19].
  - Gary Vee's social listening: if 5 people ask the same question in a few hours, thousands have it, so answer it immediately without saying "so many people asked" [2:37:34]–[2:38:40].
- **Make the audience the hero**: share community wins, transformations and case studies publicly. Example: record an Instagram video call with someone who DM'd a success. It won't be top-performing, but it invites more success stories [2:38:57]–[2:40:35].
- **UGC**: prompt the audience to share a story or insight from your lessons, then stitch their videos. A "perpetual cycle of engagement". "Even people that have 300 followers" count [2:40:35]–[2:41:26].
- **Three pillars of community content** [2:41:26]:
  1. **Educational**: solve their biggest problems.
  2. **Conversational**: make them feel heard; two-way.
  3. **Social proof**: showcase community wins.
- **Turning viewers into community members** [2:42:14]:
  1. **Make engagement the goal, not just views.** Ask for a fire emoji, or invite debate on a counter-norm take ("I'm going to be engaging with some of you") [2:42:29]–[2:43:17].
  2. **"please respond and reward participation"** [2:43:17]. Most people asking how to raise engagement aren't replying to the 3 people already commenting. Replies signal to newcomers that commenting gets a response.
  3. **Inside jokes and shared language**: use existing niche lingo ("gym bros have bulking season", Harley jokes about Sportster riders) or create your own [2:44:21]–[2:45:10].

**2.17 Scaling: platform-native content by platform-owning creators** [2:45:10]–[2:59:11]
- Cross-posting the same clips everywhere gets you to a ceiling. To break through, give the **same source media to several editors**, each cutting natively for their platform. "the meat of the video stays roughly the same, but the entry point... the hook might be different" [2:47:23]–[2:48:10].
- **Assigning platforms** [2:49:14]–[2:51:25]:
  - Rank 6 platforms. The best editor owns #1 and #4, the second #2 and #5, the third #3 and #6.
  - Each spends **85%** on their primary platform and **15%** experimenting on their secondary.
  - Example "Angela": about 7 Instagram-native pieces and 1–2 TikTok pieces a week.
  - Result on an already-huge team ("billions of impressions every year"): **1.5×** [2:51:41].
- **Why it works:**
  - Focus instead of "vanilla content".
  - Creative and data sit close together, the "beautiful marriage of creative and data" [2:53:36].
  - Team retention via a performance flywheel.
  - The audience gets a reason to follow on several platforms. "Anytime that you're posting the same content at the same time to all the different platforms, you deincentivize your audience to follow you on all these platforms." [2:54:41]
  - It trains strategists and future leaders [2:55:13]–[2:56:31].
- **Story:** when Gary assigned him TikTok (at 300K), he spent the first week making nothing, "eight hours a day for a whole week straight just scrolling", taking notes [2:48:27]–[2:48:58].
- **Matching creators to platforms** [2:56:48]–[2:59:11]:
  - **TikTok**: understands current humour, trends and the platform's "own language".
  - **LinkedIn**: deep understanding of the subject matter.
  - **YouTube**: someone who consumes a lot of YouTube and follows creators closely, and notices shifts (e.g. the vlog resurgence, and how a 2025 vlog differs from a 2015 one). Not someone who only watches Netflix and Instagram.

**2.18 Data: the multiplier sheet** [2:59:11]–[3:05:21]
- Problem: "this got 400K views" means nothing without a baseline [2:59:26].
- "I like to create what we call a multiplier sheet" [3:00:13], in Google Sheets. "directionally correct, not scientifically accurate" [3:00:30].
- **Steps:**
  1. Per platform (and ideally per format: reels, carousels, statics), take the last **75–90 days** of posts.
  2. Use the **median, not the mean**, because outliers skew it. That median is the benchmark [3:00:45]–[3:01:47].
  3. Report each post as a multiple: 150K ÷ 100K = **1.5×** [3:01:47]–[3:02:22].
  4. Wait **~11 days** after posting before reporting. Content increasingly "pops" around day 7 [3:02:38]–[3:03:11].
  5. Share the sheets across platform owners. A 3× Instagram clip is a candidate to test on TikTok. Calibrate crossover: 50K on TikTok against a 10K TikTok median is a 5× [3:03:11]–[3:04:01].
  6. Update the benchmark **monthly**, so the bar rises with your actual performance [3:04:33].
- For YouTube he points to existing tools: "one of 10.com" *(inferred: 1of10)* and "view stats" *(inferred: ViewStats)* [3:04:49]. He knew of no equivalent for Instagram or TikTok (Feb 2025).

**2.19 Data culture and other scaling rules** [3:05:21]–[3:09:21]
- Data as the team's "god". **90% of content backed by data, ~10% new ideas** [3:05:37]. Hire a "data nerd" [3:06:41].
- **Brick-by-brick list**: "it's not a boulder that builds a building. It's brick by brick." [3:06:56] Keep an internal list of the specific things that improve your content outcomes. It's niche-specific, and becomes an onboarding and training tool [3:07:13]–[3:07:44].
- **"if it doesn't change your actions, don't track it"** [3:08:00]. Track 2–4 metrics that indicate what to do next [3:08:32].
- **"the social media manager. We don't need that."** [3:08:48] The people who own a platform should do the posting and managing, so they stay tied to the data.

**2.20 Content machine: batching and the 5-step assembly line** [3:09:21]–[3:12:49]
- Real-time content (film within 24 h of the idea) is powerful but often unrealistic, so batch: block a Thursday or Friday [3:09:55]–[3:10:26].
- **Assembly line:**
  1. **Ideation**: research and brainstorm.
  2. **Creation**: script and film.
  3. **Editing**: optimised per platform by the platform owners.
  4. **Distribution**: post *and engage*.
  5. **Analyse**: review and note improvements [3:10:42]–[3:12:49].
- **On engagement:** "I would encourage you highly it should be you" [3:11:29], even if that means replying to only ~5 comments a week rather than the team replying to everything. Workaround: a clearly labelled team account (e.g. a "Team Gary Vee" account answering every comment, with no pretending to be Gary) [3:12:00].

**2.21 Experimentation: content hackathons and 70/20/10** [3:12:49]–[3:20:27]
- Teams stop experimenting as comfort sets in, but "the algorithm doesn't get comfortable". "What got you to where you are now will not get you to the next level." [3:13:20] "a lot of times true innovation has no data to back it" [3:13:54].
- **Content hackathon** (monthly or quarterly) [3:14:28]–[3:17:46]:
  - 9:00 a.m.: 15–30 min kickoff giving context and a "safe space": "I don't care how anything you make today performs."
  - Make only **1–2 pieces**, something new that will "either... absolutely crush or completely flop".
  - Lunch is paid (gift cards).
  - Create from 9 a.m. to 4 p.m.; upload to a shared slide deck by 4:30.
  - 1–1.5 h presentations where each person explains *why*.
  - The **team votes** for the most innovative piece, which then runs publicly, with a prize (e.g. an Amazon gift card).
  - Reward innovation, not performance. His claim: some top formats that ran "for months or even years" came from hackathons.
- **70/20/10 framework** [3:18:19]:
  - **70%** proven content ("This is the bread and butter").
  - **20%** small iterations on what works: new hooks, a new CTA, a new format on the same subject.
  - **10%** pure experiments: new formats, topics, messaging or creative approaches.
  - Most teams get stuck on the 70%. "comfort doesn't build iconic brands" [3:19:07].
- **Scaling mindset** [3:19:40]: people quit too early; it's iteration, not perfection ("Progress over perfection"); let the audience determine what is great.

### Section 3: Team

**2.22 Team principles** [3:20:27]–[3:23:41]
- The team is "a multiplier of the vision".
- Hire for **culture over skills** ("a lot easier to train skills than culture fit").
- Hire around **platform goals**, and to fit your creative process (e.g. off-the-cuff talent needs people who ask good questions).
- "We hire to solve problems." [3:22:21] Not to fill roles.
- A bad hire "massively slows you down" and costs months and money.

**2.23 Define needs before hiring (hire for the constraint)** [3:23:41]–[3:33:24]
- Filming lots but nothing edited → hire an editor. Editors idle → hire a videographer or content strategist [3:24:15]–[3:24:47].
- **Three questions** [3:25:02]–[3:26:53]:
  1. What tasks take the most time away from high-value work?
  2. What's slowing down execution?
  3. What content opportunities are missed due to lack of resources?
- "prioritize hiring for bottlenecks over convenience". Hire for leverage, not to offload busywork [3:27:26].
- **Platform-specific skills**: a Netflix editor ≠ a YouTube editor; a documentary DP ≠ a vlog shooter; a brochure designer ≠ a thumbnail designer. "a lot of YouTube thumbnail best practices slap in the face of traditional graphic design best practices" [3:28:16]–[3:29:53].
- **No generalist social media manager**: "If they're a generalist, if they are great at all of the platforms, they suck at all of the platforms." [3:30:10] Hire channel-specific managers.
  - If LinkedIn drives results, hire a **LinkedIn writer** (not a generic copywriter) before a video editor, as he did in 2025 [3:31:14].
  - Progression: YouTube editor (2× output) → short-form editor repurposing long form (2–5× impressions) → **platform** strategist (not "social media strategist") [3:31:46]–[3:32:34].
- Key takeaway: "always hire for the highest impact content first" [3:33:06].

**2.24 Define the role, then the Four Rs job description** [3:33:24]–[3:41:58]
- Spell out ancillary needs (e.g. only filming 4–8 a.m.) or you waste interviews [3:33:56].
- **Role questions** [3:34:44]–[3:36:35]:
  1. Is this role about creating, managing or analysing content?
  2. Single-platform specialist, or (early on) "a specialist who is willing to play the role of a generalist"?
  3. How does the role contribute to the bigger picture?
- "What do you get with vague job descriptions? Vague candidates." [3:36:50]
- **Four Rs** [3:37:08]–[3:40:54], illustrated with his brand-director JD:
  1. **Role**: the contribution and function.
  2. **Responsibilities**: daily, weekly, monthly, quarterly tasks.
  3. **Requirements**: skills and experience (e.g. "three plus years of experience growing a personal brand").
  4. **Results**: specific output metrics (e.g. "A minimum of two videos per month" on YouTube; LinkedIn "at least two written and two video posts per week"). "Think of it like responsibilities is the input and results is the output."
- **Plus:** communication cadence and expectations (e.g. a 7:30 a.m. Slack reply), and **core values** [3:40:54]–[3:41:58].

**2.25 The hiring funnel** [3:41:58]–[4:02:26]
His hiring mistakes all came from rushing and ignoring red flags. Look for an **80% candidate** and discuss whether the missing 20% is trainable [3:42:46]–[3:43:36].
1. **Application**: CV plus an **optional video** (personality doesn't show in a CV). Job-hopping (10 jobs in 5 years) is a signal to explore, not an automatic rejection. His case: a creative director's multicam self-intro video won the job [3:43:36]–[3:46:16].
2. **Screening call**: salary, location (in person / remote / hybrid), communication and travel expectations, plus **2–3 light technical questions**. These also expose candidates reading AI-generated answers, which he saw "five times in the last month" [3:46:16]–[3:48:08].
3. **Technical interview**: strategic knowledge **and** ability to execute. Ask how they'd *innovate* on your current style, not whether they can copy it. Many editors come from a "checklist army" without ownership [3:48:08]–[3:50:36].
4. **Technical assessment**: mirrors the real job. E.g. **72 hours + ~1 hour of raw footage** to create a new short-form style. It tests time management, delivery and communication, execution vs interview talk, and whether they show their work. Give everyone the same assessment [3:50:36]–[3:52:45].
5. **Culture interview**: scenario questions, e.g. how they respond to direct feedback in front of 5+ people, or to a non-manager demanding they drop their work, or a CEO's terse style [3:52:45]–[3:56:48]. His case: hired Trevor Odum at "6 out of 10 in his editing" but "10 out of 10" on culture [3:54:53].
6. **Final interview**: with the most senior person the hire will interact with. Pokes holes in open questions; nothing should remain unresolved. Their questions to you reveal a lot [3:56:48]–[3:58:55].
- **One-person-team version:** combine steps (screening + technical, then the assessment, then culture + final), or let a trusted agency run the technical interview [3:59:11]–[4:00:34].
- **Why it works:** it removes bad hires early (the optional video filters out mass or AI applicants), ensures platform alignment ("Are they a great editor or are they a great YouTube editor?"), ensures culture fit (avoids "an anchor rather than a rocket"), and speeds things up [4:00:34]–[4:02:26].

**2.26 Tryout funnel (editors, designers, strategists only)** [4:02:26]–[4:06:11]
1. Monday: announce and open registration; close Thursday 12 p.m. PT.
2. Thursday ~5 p.m.: email the raw footage. The brief: edit a **12–20 min YouTube video**, plus **thumbnail + title**, plus a **short**. Due Monday 9 a.m.
3. The same funnel then runs with the assessment first: screening → technical → culture → final.

Prioritisation is part of the test. His numbers: ~1,500 registered, ~220–250 submitted. It works only where one shared source asset can scale to hundreds of candidates; not for videographers.

**2.27 Culture over perfection; platform strengths** [4:06:11]–[4:14:32]
- Technical skills train fast; hunger, empathy and adaptability don't. His case: Jason Leva *(transcript spelling)*, whose edit wasn't top-10 but who took direct feedback "so graciously". One of his best hires. "Hire for mindset and train for execution." [4:08:40]
- **What fits each platform, and the red flags** [4:08:56]–[4:14:32]:
  - **TikTok/Instagram**: immersed in trends; fast-paced editing; knows how to "open and close loops to retain attention"; spots gaps in your niche. **Red flag:** "overly focused on high production value without understanding what it takes to attract attention, a strong hook" [4:10:50].
  - **LinkedIn**: concise, high-value writing that **sparks conversation in the comments** (engagement shows the post to others). **Red flag:** formal, corporate writing. "The more human and the more personable you are... the more you will stand out".
  - **YouTube long form**: knows the "opening 15 to 30 seconds is the most crucial part"; builds tension, stakes and open loops; spreads value through the video instead of front-loading it all in the first 3 minutes ("YouTube loves watch time"). **Red flag (videographer):** over-traditional, perfect-lighting, primes-only shooters for a run-and-gun vlog setup [4:13:13]–[4:14:32].

**2.28 Start lean, grow intentionally** [4:14:32]–[4:21:38]
- A small "seal team six style team" beats a bloated one. Specialists wear several hats early, e.g. one person who films and edits; decide which skill matters more [4:14:50]–[4:16:12].
- **Why:** avoids payroll before content ROI is proven; keeps the team nimble ("speedboat" vs "oil tanker"); builds cross-role context and cover for sickness or departures [4:16:29]–[4:19:08].
- **Role-expansion framework:**
  1. Start with multi-role hires.
  2. Document and define the process.
  3. Specialise as you scale.
- Splitting roles protects flow: an editor pulled out to film loses "another 30 to 45 minutes" getting back into flow [4:19:26]–[4:21:22].

**2.29 Full-time vs contractor/agency (6 factors)** [4:21:38]–[4:32:20]

| Factor | Full-time employee | Contractor / agency |
|---|---|---|
| Budget | Higher long-term cost (salary, benefits, training) | Cost-effective for short-term or specialised work. Should come "batteries included" (no training paid by you). His motion-graphics artist Michael is a contractor |
| Workload | Ongoing daily tasks | Project-based, flexible or temporary (e.g. filming 1–2×/month, or an event needing 4 shooters) |
| Expertise | Deep knowledge and long-term investment; you train them | Should arrive as the expert. Hiring agencies for 3–6 months to "school us" is "like a fast pass" |
| Speed/agility | 60–90 days to get up to speed, then continuity | Immediate. But agencies churn: "seven different editors over the course of six to 12 months" |
| Brand consistency | Easier: they're in internal meetings and get constant feedback | Needs the most oversight. Agencies have shown imagery of people the brand wanted no association with |
| Scalability | Better long term: they grow into leaders who carry the team's DNA | Good for **testing a theory**, e.g. 3–6 months of LinkedIn with a contractor building a playbook before a full-time hire |

**2.30 Three growth stages** [4:32:20]–[4:38:16] (general advice, "not... doctrine")
1. **0–1 year**: mostly contractors and agencies. Stay lean and test platforms ("the best platforms to be on and then there's the best platforms for you to be on"). The founder stays heavily involved in content and strategy.
2. **1–3 years**: bring critical roles in-house (e.g. if YouTube brings 70–80% of qualified leads, hire an in-house editor). Shrink the agency to consulting, ideation, scripting and packaging.
3. **3+ years**: bring all critical, high-volume roles in-house; speed and communication jump. Keep agencies for specialised work, platform access (e.g. a media buyer's relationship with Meta reps), and temporary sprints, so you avoid hiring and then firing.

**2.31 Onboarding** [4:38:48]–[4:55:01]
- **30-60-90 plan**, ideally sent before day one (he once got his 5 days early):
  - **30 days**: orientation and core learning. Vision, values, workflows, tools (Asana / ClickUp, content calendar), small tasks for early wins, regular check-ins. An editor shadows a senior editor, reviews the top 5 videos and why they worked, and does rough cuts that won't be published.
  - **60 days**: ownership. They execute their core responsibilities independently and build their own workflow within the team process; their work and communication are reviewed with feedback.
  - **90 days**: full integration with minimal supervision, plus results against KPIs (e.g. a YouTube editor's watch time vs average, and the "rate of progress"). Then a **90-day review**: praise specifics and correct problems now. "If you do not tell them now... That is your fault." [4:47:10]
- **Other components** [4:47:58]–[4:51:29]:
  1. A clear plan and materials before day one (handbook, vision deck, strategy deck).
  2. An assigned mentor or onboarding buddy.
  3. Regular check-ins: in month one, "three 10-minute meetings, Monday, Wednesday, Friday". Celebrate wins (he high-fived a hire who spoke up on day one).
  4. *(Bonus)* An onboarding hub, even just a Google Drive folder.
- Keep role descriptions updated. Avoid overlapping ownership: two people owning a task means nobody does it. Set KPIs [4:51:29]–[4:53:23].
- **Results:** faster ramp-up, retention (losing an employee costs "like two and a half times what they actually costed you"), and more initiative [4:53:23]–[4:54:45].

**2.32 Developing and retaining the team** [4:55:01]–[5:21:48]
- **Culture of ownership vs the "checklist army"**: "If you are the only one making decisions, you don't have a team." [4:56:38] [5:20:08]
  - Don't solve their problems. Ask "how would you handle this? If I got hit by a bus...". Ask for solutions, not problems; then ask which of their options they'd pick.
  - Give autonomy with accountability, and let non-critical mistakes happen. His creative-director case: he answered texted questions for 3 months with no improvement; once he flipped the questions back, the person improved [4:57:10]–[5:01:13].
- **Teaching as a leadership hack**: rotating, team-led weekly trainings. They build mastery ("If you can't actually explain something, you don't fully understand it") and reveal hidden strengths (e.g. someone passionate about sound design or storytelling becomes the go-to) [5:01:13]–[5:04:39].
- **Mentorship loop**: pair by skill seniority, not tenure, both ways [5:04:39].
- **Align work with long-term goals.** Creatives "don't like to present" is often learned, because they were never given the chance [5:05:29]–[5:07:20].
- **One-on-one questions:** What's your long-term career goal? Where do you see yourself after this? What skills do you want to develop? E.g. an editor who loves motion graphics gets fancier lower thirds, transitions and text animation to work on [5:07:36]–[5:08:57].
- **Promote specialisation.** His case: Trevor, as a short-form editor (~14–16 months in), considered switching to long form. He stayed and became senior editor, then lead editor managing 3 editors at 22–23. His career is following the same "accordion" (wide → narrow → wide) [5:12:27]–[5:16:21].
- **Invest in education** (e.g. a YouTube retention-editing workshop from someone who worked with MrBeast). Send 1–2 people and have them **turn it into a team training** [5:16:36]–[5:17:25].
- **One-on-ones:** workload and blockers ("Can I bulldoze through any roadblocks"), plus revisiting long-term goals [5:17:58]–[5:19:52].
- **Section takeaways** [5:20:08]:
  1. Push ownership down.
  2. Make leadership (teaching) a habit.
  3. Tie their growth to the company's growth.
  4. Go deep, not wide.
  5. Structured check-ins: "If you choose not to make time for feedback, you'll eventually have to make time for hiring." [5:21:15]

**2.33 Team culture** [5:21:48]–[5:38:58]
- **Core values**: hire on alignment, publicly recognise and reward value-aligned actions, and lead by example. A youth-pastor lesson: "whatever you do, they're going to do 10 times" [5:24:11]. Patagonia example: hiring for sustainability, grants, 1% of sales, closing on powder days [5:25:15]–[5:26:33].
- **Transparency**: explain the *why* of decisions (his case: explaining why a 6-day, 13 h/day edit was killed at the talent's request). Own your mistakes immediately. Communicate pivots early [5:26:33]–[5:30:17].
- **Care**: notice what people love (a Lego set for a birthday); send soup or juice when someone's sick [5:30:17]–[5:31:20].
- **Growth culture**: tie contributions to company growth, check career goals, give stretch opportunities. "Trust begets trust." [5:33:15]
- **Maker/manager schedule** (credited to Paul Graham) [5:34:06]–[5:38:42]:
  - Makers (editors, designers) lose 30 min to 1 h of flow per interruption.
  - His set-up: **maker time 7 a.m.–12 p.m.**, **manager time 12–5 p.m.**
  - Monday = meeting day; Wednesday = a full maker day; Tuesday/Thursday/Friday = maker mornings, manager 2–5 p.m.
  - Teams push to get a full maker day.

**2.34 Remote vs in-person vs hybrid (6 factors)** [5:38:58]–[6:01:16]
- **Flexibility**: remote high (and can justify below-market pay); in-person low; hybrid moderate.
- **Collaboration**: remote needs clear digital tools and rules, with few "happy accidents"; in-person makes real-time collaboration easier (vs notes in Frame.io, which he still rates "better than 99% of alternatives").
- **Productivity**: varies by person in both. He works better at home (VaynerMedia's open floor meant constant shoulder taps). Hybrid lets you tailor days per person.

### Section 4: Monetise

**2.35 Trust before transactions** [6:04:21]–[6:06:17]
"trust is the currency that preeds *(precedes)* the transaction" [6:04:37]. Before monetising, a brand should:
1. Provide an unbelievable amount of value up front.
2. Demonstrate credibility and expertise (become "the go-to authority").
3. Establish a relationship: engagement matters, and then "sales become effortless" without pushing scarcity or urgency.

He acknowledges that bills sometimes force earlier monetisation [6:05:09].

**2.36 Five monetisation models** [6:06:32]–[6:09:45]
1. **Services**: agency, consulting, coaching. High-touch; fits deep expertise that people already ask for.
2. **Products**: digital (ebooks, templates, playbooks, courses; "very high margin") or physical (books, apparel). Lean digital early.
3. **Affiliates & sponsorships**: only things you actually use and believe in. A bad product paired with you consistently creates a "bad brand effect" (his branding definition again).
4. **Community & memberships**: recurring revenue (exclusive content, masterminds, groups, paid newsletters); a hub for super-fans.
5. **Ads & content monetisation** (YouTube ad revenue, podcast and newsletter ads): pair it with another model. Small at first; covers production costs.

**Pro tip:** check email, DMs and comments for what the audience is already asking for [6:09:29].

**2.37 Share the knowledge, sell the execution** [6:09:45]–[6:12:10]
- "the more value you give, the more demand you create" [6:10:01].
- Free content should be so valuable that people feel compelled to act, then realise the execution is hard.
- His case: watched ~100 YouTube videos on swapping the exhaust on his Harley Road King, then paid the Harley dealership to install it [6:10:34]–[6:11:40].

**2.38 Offer stack (start with one offer, refine, then expand)** [6:12:10]–[6:14:05]
1. **Free content**: trust and awareness.
2. **Lead magnet** (mini-course, SOP, checklist, playbook): captures emails and phone numbers, so you're "not just beholden to the algorithms".
3. **Low ticket**: **$10–$100** (ebooks, mini-courses, templates). Removes the entry barrier.
4. **Mid ticket**: **$500–$5,000** (group coaching, memberships, in-depth courses).
5. **High ticket**: **$10,000+** (1:1 consulting, agency, done-for-you).

Most successful brands don't start with high ticket. "Don't start with five different offers. You're going to confuse your audience." [6:12:27]

**2.39 Let content do the selling** [6:14:05]–[6:15:27]
"People hate feeling sold to, but they love buying from someone they trust."
1. Teach a valuable lesson, maybe with a **soft CTA**.
2. Share client success stories.
3. Document your process (behind the scenes).
4. Address common objections.
5. Use lead magnets to capture data.

His example: not "Buy my ebook on personal branding" but "I helped John go from 0 to 10k followers in 4 and 1/2 months using this exact strategy. If you want the full system, I break it down in my ebook... link in my bio." [6:15:12]

**2.40 Play the long game** [6:15:27]–[6:19:02]
1. **Deliver so much value people feel guilty not buying.** Give the actual frameworks: "Don't do the watered down version, not some teaser" [6:16:18]. The reaction he wants: "if this is what they're giving away for free, what … are they charging for?" [6:16:33]
2. **Build a system people come back to**: recurring formats ("challenges, weekly breakdowns"), playlists, content categorised by topic, easy to binge. "think more like Netflix, a series" instead of a one-hit viral video [6:16:50]–[6:17:23].
3. **Protect your reputation at all costs.** It's "way harder to rebuild trust than it is to maintain trust". The test before any monetisation move: "will this increase trust with my audience or decrease trust?" [6:17:56]

- [Filming] Film at your best-energy time of day; protect content time like a meeting; someone behind the camera nodding or giving a thumbs up = real-time feedback. [L05]
- [Growth] Prioritise 2–3 platforms; grow one at a time while the others sit in "maintenance mode" (Eye of Sauron); avoid single-platform risk. [L05]
- [Growth] Short form = awareness, long form = conversion. Judge content by conversions (leads, sales, sign-ups), not vanity metrics. [L05]
- [Growth] Waterfall: one long-form piece → shorts, quote posts, a written post (reused as a reel caption), carousels; "easily over 10 pieces". [L05]
- [Growth] Multiplier sheet: per platform and format, the median of the last 75–90 days = benchmark; report each post as a multiple (150K ÷ 100K = 1.5×); wait ~11 days; update monthly. [L05]
- [Growth] Monthly top-10% review (last 90 days): keep only the 1–2 traits shared by all top posts. "If it doesn't change your actions, don't track it" (2–4 metrics). [L05]
- [Growth] 70/20/10: 70% proven formats, 20% iterations (new hook, CTA or format), 10% pure experiments. [L05]
- [Growth] Engagement: reply to the commenters you already have, personally; use inside jokes and shared niche language; take ideas from repeated DM/comment questions, not "what should I make?" polls. [L05]
- [Growth] A CTA doesn't have to sell: "comment your biggest takeaway and how you'll apply it" (vs comment-keyword-to-DM, see CLASHES). [L05]
- [Business] Trust before transactions; "share the knowledge, sell the execution". Offer ladder: free content → lead magnet (email) → low $10–100 → mid $500–5K → high $10K+; start with one offer. [L05]
- [Business] Five monetisation paths: services, products (digital = high margin), affiliates/sponsors (only what you use), community/memberships, ads (an add-on). Test for any deal: "will this increase or decrease trust?" [L05]
- [Business] Team: hire for the bottleneck, platform-specific specialists over a generalist "social media manager", culture over skills; contractors in year 0–1, core roles in-house in years 1–3, everything in-house after year 3. [L05]
